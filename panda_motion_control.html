🐼 Overview
The Panda Motion Control module leverages Reinforcement Learning (RL) to enable the robot to learn complex locomotion and manipulation behaviors autonomously. By combining RL with physics-based simulation and real-world transfer, the system learns efficient motion primitives that generalize across tasks. This approach eliminates the need for hand-crafted controllers, allowing the Panda platform to autonomously discover effective solutions for complex robotic challenges.
⚡ Challenge
  • Autonomous Locomotion: Train the robot to walk and navigate across diverse terrain through learned policies, adapting to environmental dynamics in real time.
  • Tree Climbing: Develop specialized climbing behaviors that allow the robot to ascend tree trunks using learned limb coordination and force control strategies.
  • Human Interaction: Enable safe and intuitive interactions with humans through contact-aware control policies that respond appropriately to external forces and gestures.

🧩 Fune Tune Simulation Model
Introduction
To ensure efficient and reliable deployment, the robot’s behavior in simulation must closely resemble its physical behavior in the real world. Instead of pursuing perfect physical accuracy, the goal is to achieve behavioral similarity — meaning the robot should respond to commands and environmental interactions in a comparable way across both domains.
(1) Drop Test
Test description
A scripted drop (fixed height) is used to measure impact response and settling. Compare peak acceleration, bounce, and settling time between simulation and real robot to guide inertia and contact parameter adjustments.
(2) Arm Drop Test
Test description
Arm drop tests focus on joint-level impact and compliance. Use these runs to tune joint stiffness and damping so that joint transient responses match the hardware (rise/settle times and overshoot), rather than matching exact numeric values.
🕹️ Low Level Control
Training Objective
The goal of the low-level policy is to perform precise and stable position control for the leg's end-effector. In this training setup, the policy is responsible for driving all joint motors so that the foot can accurately reach the commanded target position in task space. The entire training process is designed to ensure that, given a desired end-effector position, the policy can consistently generate smooth and reliable joint-position commands that track the target without jitter or instability.
Observations (obs):
  • joint_pos — current joint angles
  • joint_vel — joint angular velocities
  • pose_command — target foot position in task space
  • last_actions — previous joint commands for smoothness and stability
Actions:
  • Joint position targets for all leg motors (joint position control)
Commands:
  • End-effector (foot) position in 3D space (Relative to the Shoulder Part)
Issues and Solutions
Encountered Issue Solution Implemented
End-effector constrained to always face downward, limiting natural motion Removed the orientation constraint and widened joint range of motion
Full Panda USD model caused unnecessary restrictions and reduced leg flexibility Simplified USD to a single-arm model for focused training
Removing the orientation reward caused the end-effector to rotate freely (position reached but pose uncontrolled) Reintroduced orientation tracking reward and expanded allowable Euler command range
Increasing number of environments resulted in jitter and longer training without performance gain Reduced number of environments to maintain stability and shorten training time
Early and heavy penalties on action_rate and joint_vel suppressed exploration and caused unstable behavior Introduced curriculum to gradually increase penalties and tuned reward weights
Joint motion became unstable and noisy under improper penalty scaling Rebalanced penalty terms and progressively adjusted them through curriculum
Model Results Summary
  • • The model demonstrates stable and smooth joint motions, with minimal jitter during control.
  • • End-effector tracking (position & orientation) remains within a consistent and narrow range, indicating reliable task-space control.
  • • The action rate reward decreases and stabilizes, showing smoother and less abrupt motor commands.
  • • Joint velocity behavior improves over time, reflecting better regulation of joint motion.
  • • Entropy converges to a stable plateau, meaning the policy has transitioned from exploration to a confident, stable solution.
  • • Mean reward becomes more consistent as training progresses, showing the model has found a reliable strategy for position tracking.

🧭 High Level Control Ongoing
Training Objective
To ensure efficient and reliable deployment, the robot’s behavior in simulation must closely resemble its physical behavior in the real world. Instead of pursuing perfect physical accuracy, the goal is to achieve behavioral similarity — meaning the robot should respond to commands and environmental interactions in a comparable way across both domains.
Observations (obs):
  • base_lin_vel — current robot linear velocity
  • base_ang_vel — current robot angular velocity
  • projected_gravity — robot base tiltness
  • velocity_commands — velocity command
  • joint_pos — current joint angles
  • joint_vel — joint angular velocities
  • last_actions — previous joint commands for smoothness and stability
Actions:
  • Four legs end-effector positions ➡︎ Position + Quaternion
Commands:
  • Velocity Command ➡︎ Full Robot Velocity
📝 Note: This demonstrates that the high-level policy needs to observe both whole-body information and low-level control information.
Issues and Solutions
I have identified a severe misalignment between the kinematic zero-point and the command zero-point, which significantly hinders learning:
  • • Kinematic Zero-Point (The Good Stance): When all actuators are assigned a position of zero, the robot assumes a stable, vertical standing posture. This is the optimal physical reference pose for locomotion.
  • • Command Zero-Point (The Bad Stance): However, when the High-Level Policy commands a zero End-Effector target, the Low-Level Policy (LLP) outputs a non-zero joint action that results in the splayed, low-to-the-ground posture.
Implication: The LLP's trained policy does not interpret End Effector Target as "stay at the kinematic zero-point." This means the High-Level Policy must learn a large, non-zero bias just to get the robot into a starting position, drastically increasing the complexity of the exploration space and leading directly to the observed high entropy.
